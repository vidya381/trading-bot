### Part 1 — What Workers AI offers for a SECOND, LARGER call, verified

Re-read from Cloudflare's live documentation rather than carried forward.
**Nothing changes in kind.** Context window **24,000 tokens**, text-generation
rate limit **300 req/min**, **$0.011 per 1,000 Neurons** with **10,000/day
free**, this model at **26,668 neurons per M input tokens and 204,805 per M
output**. Workers: **no wall-clock duration limit** for HTTP triggers, CPU time
**5 min on Paid (30 s default)** and network waiting excluded.

**Real prompt sizes, measured by building both prompts over a full 1,440-candle
window** rather than estimated:

| prompt | characters | evidence items | ~tokens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Assess | 15,800 | 54 | ~3,950 |
| Derive (grid) | **23,383** | **75** | ~5,846 |
| Derive (dca) | **23,812** | **75** | ~5,953 |

So **Derive is ~1.5× Assess, not 2×**, and lands at roughly **a quarter of the
context window**; with `max_tokens: 4096` the worst case is ~10k of 24k.
Bucketing at 24 still holds and no model change was needed.

**Cost roughly TRIPLES per candidate rather than doubling**, because Derive's
output is much larger than its input growth: Assess ≈ 166 neurons, Derive ≈ 321
typical and ~996 if it generates to the ceiling. Per candidate both stages:
**~490 neurons (~$0.005) typical, up to ~1,270 (~$0.014)**. The figure worth

**One thing step 40's duration research does NOT cover, flagged before the

### Part 2 — Two prompts and two schemas, never one with optional fields

A grid bot and a DCA bot need different parameters, so `buildDerivePrompt`
dispatches on the strategy Stage 2 actually chose and builds exactly one
branch's field list, field contract, worked shape example and JSON schema. The
field lists were confirmed against spec 21.4 before either was built and are
**pinned literally by a test**, because that list IS the spec quotation.

The rejected alternative — one schema carrying every field of both strategies
with the irrelevant ones optional — fails specifically: **an optional field is a
field the model MAY fill in**, so a DCA proposal would be free to carry an
`upperBound` and a human would review a parameter set mentioning a bound no DCA
bot has. It would also make "which fields are required" a runtime question, and
`requireExactFields` — the check that makes a missing field a total refusal —
would have nothing exact to require.

A test asserts the two field lists **intersect in exactly `["stopLossPct"]`**
(mandatory for both strategies, section 6.1). A growing intersection is the
signature of the conditional design quietly collapsing into a universal one.

**What the two share is everything else**, and sharing is by CODE rather than by
convention:

- **`collectBundleEvidence`**, extracted from `buildAssessPrompt`, so the bundle
  evidence in a Derive prompt is produced by the same function that produced it
  for Assess. A test asserts **every Assess id appears in the Derive table
  rendering byte-identical `value` and `source`**. Without that, `candles.range_pct`
  in a Derive citation and in an Assess claim could be two different numbers, and
  the human checking one against the other would have no way to know.
- **`SHARED_GROUNDING_RULES`**, the six rules 21.5 requires, as constants with
  numbering applied at composition time by `numberedRules`. Each stage supplies
  only the rules genuinely its own — Assess's *"do not propose any parameters"*
  and Derive's *"the strategy is already decided"* are exact opposites.
- **`readModelAnswer` / `requireExactFields` / `resolveCitations`**, extracted
  with an injected `ParseRefusal` so the LOGIC has one implementation while each
  stage keeps its own error class. `derive-parse.ts` never throws an error named
  `AssessParseError`.

**The extraction changed no byte of the Assess prompt, and that is proven rather
than assumed.** A new test pins the whole eight-rule numbered block literally, in
the form it had before the extraction — the existing `toContain` assertions would
have passed against a reworded rule that kept its capitalised phrases.

**Stage 2's choice is an input, never a question.** The prompt states the
strategy as decided and forbids arguing with it; a response naming a different
one is `strategy_disagreement`, a **total refusal**. 21.2 says a divergence
between stages is a bug, and weighing it — taking the later answer, or surfacing
it as a disagreement for the human to consider — would turn a fault into a
feature and put a strategy choice in front of a human that no stage is
accountable for.

**Every proposed number carries a citation**, by Stage 2's mechanism. Each field
arrives as `{value, citations}`; the citations resolve against the ids this run's
prompt really emitted; an empty or invented citation is fatal for the whole
proposal. **`value` is never inspected by the parser** — it goes to the real
decoder untouched, which is what makes the decoder reuse real rather than
nominal.

### Part 3 — Three validation layers, and why the third is SMALLER than it looks

They run in the real create-bot path's own order, confirmed from source:

1. **`decodeGridParams` / `decodeDcaParams`** — the exact decoders
   `POST /api/bots` runs on a human's own submission, handed the model's
   `parameters` object with nothing added, removed, renamed or coerced, plus the
   same two literals `createBot` adds.
2. **`validateGridParams` / `validateDcaParams`** — the exact validators
   `BotInstance.createGrid`/`create` run before any capital is reserved.
3. **Only what neither of those checks.**

**21.5 requirement 3 names an inverted grid range and a zero percentage as sanity
bounds, and BOTH ARE ALREADY REFUSED BY LAYER 2, BY THE REAL CODE** —
`buildLevels` throws `upperBound must be above lowerBound`, and both validators
throw on every non-positive percentage. **No copy was added, and the omission is
the decision.** A second copy would be the duplicated risk check requirement 3's
own first bullet forbids (*"a second implementation of a risk check drifts from
the first, and the copy that drifts is the one nobody is watching"*) **and** —
because layer 3 runs last — a check that **could never fire**, which is exactly
the duplicate-key "theatre" step 37 refused: a guard that always returns clean
reads as protection while providing none.

**So the cases are tested and the tests assert WHICH LAYER refused**
(`DeriveValidationError.layer` is `"decoder" | "strategy_validator" |
"sanity_bound"`, and the real `GridError`/`DcaError` travels as `cause`). Without
that field, a future edit that dropped `validateGridParams` from the chain and
let a local copy absorb the same cases would be invisible: the same inputs would
still be refused, by different code, and every test asserting only "it refuses"
would still pass.

**This deviates from the literal instruction for this step, which asked for those
two bounds explicitly. It is recorded here as a deviation rather than absorbed
silently.**

What layer 3 genuinely adds is three things nothing upstream does: the
**minimum order floor**, the **capital headroom comparison**, and the
**capital asset** being one the account holds a ledger row for.

### The minimum-order floor: a check that would have been a permanent no-op

**Gemini publishes NO notional bounds at all** — `parseSymbolDetails` sets
`minNotional: DISABLED`, because `/v1/symbols/details/:symbol` carries none. A
check written only against `minNotional`, which is what 21.5 requirement 3's
wording literally asks for, **would pass every proposal forever on the only venue
this system runs against, while reading in review like a floor.** Gemini
publishes `min_order_size` instead, which the client maps to `minQuantity`.

So the check runs in **whichever dimension the venue actually publishes**, using
the strategy's own `quantityForLevel`/`quantityForQuote` — the same flooring
arithmetic the order path uses, so the check and the order it is about cannot
disagree by a rounding step — at the **highest price an order of that size would
be placed at**, where the implied quantity is smallest and the floor bites
hardest (grid: `upperBound`; DCA: the newest real close).

And `MinimumOrderCheck` **reports which floor held for that proposal** —
`"notional"`, `"quantity"`, `"both"` or `"none_published"`. Same instinct as
`DuplicateKeyCheck` one stage earlier: a guarantee that could not be checked is
reported, never faked.

**The DCA reference price cannot be exact and is not claimed to be.** A real
price may move above the last close before an order is placed; the binding
protection there is `validateOrder`'s filter check at order time (section 4.3),
exactly as the binding capital protection is the ledger's at creation time.

### The capital read is a PREFILL, and the word is load-bearing

`readAccountCapital` reads `capital_ledger` through the real repository and does
the subtraction `total_balance - total_allocated`. It **writes nothing, reserves
nothing, touches no `total_allocated` and holds no lock**, and 21.1 guarantees
this pipeline has no write path to one.

**The binding check remains `createBotInstanceWithCapital`'s**, which re-reads the
ledger inside a compare-and-swap at the moment a bot is created and refuses with
`insufficient_capital`. Between Derive's read and that moment there is an
arbitrarily long gap in which another bot may be created or reconciliation may
rewrite the balance, so **the headroom Derive proposes within is stale by
construction from the instant it is read** (21.5 requirement 4). If Derive
proposes 500 and the ledger has 400 by submission time, the real flow refuses —
correctly, and without needing to know this stage exists. The refusal message
says so in the open: *"clearing this check is NOT an entitlement"*.

**The subtraction is restated; the CHECK is not copied**, and the distinction was
traced rather than assumed. `src/capital/ledger.ts` exposes **no read-only
headroom function**: all three exports write, and the availability arithmetic
lives inside a closure passed to the compare-and-swap loop, reachable only by
performing an allocation. `total_balance - total_allocated` is the definition of
"available" and already appears verbatim in `AllocationAuditDetails`; comparing
it against a request and refusing is `ledger.ts`'s alone.

**A negative `available` is preserved, not clamped.** Migration 0001
deliberately permits `total_allocated > total_balance` (a losing bot), and
clamping would hide the exact state a human most needs to see before allocating
more. **Zero rows is a reported state, not an error** — `ledger.ts` creates no
row automatically, so an unseeded account genuinely has none.

### The second gather, deliberately separate from the first

`gatherDeriveContext` is a new function in `gather.ts` rather than two more
fields on `CandidateGatherBundle`, for three reasons that are all about not
charging Stage 2 for Stage 3's needs:

1. **COST.** A symbol-details read is **one uncached per-symbol request** —
   `tradability.ts` says so and warns that `selectGeneralCandidates` checks up to
   fifteen coins per run. Inside `gatherCandidateData` it would be spent on every
   candidate of every Assess run. A tripwire test proves Stage 1 never touches
   the port.
2. **ORDER.** Reading the money figure as late as possible is the right direction
   for a number 21.5 requirement 4 calls stale by construction.
3. **BLAST RADIUS.** `CandidateGatherBundle` is what Stage 2's prompt, its tests
   and the live-verified `/assess` endpoint are built on.

The discipline is unchanged: both slots are the same `GatheredInput` envelope,
both carry the producing layer's own failure, and neither failure erases the
other's result.

### Zero retries, and why the reason is stronger here than for Assess

`deriveParameters` calls the model **exactly once**, and four tests count the
calls positively — decision log 39's surviving mutant C9 taught that asserting
the error's identity says nothing about the call count.

Assess picks between two words, so a resampling loop is at worst a biased coin.
**Derive proposes nine numbers against validators**, so "retry until it
validates" would be a **search procedure whose objective function is
"parameters that squeeze through the checks"**, run by something with no stake in
whether they are good — and the set it eventually returned would look identical
in an audit record to one the model proposed cleanly on the first try.

### Four refusals BEFORE the model is called

No price history, an unreadable capital ledger, no headroom on any asset, and
unreadable symbol filters. Each refuses with its own code and a call count of
**zero**, so a refusal costs no inference. Each is the absence of something the
question is *about* rather than a quality judgement (still Stage 4's).

**The filters one is the one that would be easiest to wave through**, and it is
refused for `checkSpotInstrument`'s reason: a floor that did not arrive is not a
floor that said zero. Proceeding would present a parameter set as validated when
the one check requirement 3 names that nothing else in this system performs
before an order is placed could not run at all.

**A structural property found during mutation testing and worth recording:
four fail-open mutants WOULD NOT COMPILE.** The `GatheredInput` discriminated
union means you cannot fail open by deleting a guard — you must actively write a
fabricated value at a second site. That is the type system enforcing 21.5
requirement 6 rather than a test doing it.

| run | Assess | Derive | model time, chain |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **13,080 ms** | **28,632 ms** | **41,712 ms** |
| 2 | **41,511 ms** | **46,969 ms** | **88,480 ms** |

**This supersedes the band recorded in entries 37, 39 and 40.** Assess alone had
been five samples spanning **10,546–20,326 ms**, and entry 40 recorded that a
real end-to-end call had *not* widened it. **It has now.** Seven Assess samples
span **10,546–41,511 ms** — a **~3.9× spread**, where it was ~2×. Derive's two
samples are **28,632 and 46,969 ms**, both above every Assess sample ever taken,
which is consistent with a ~1.5× prompt and a much larger answer but is **two
data points and not a distribution.**

**Run 2 spent nearly ninety seconds in model calls for ONE candidate.**

**This sharpens the synchronous-vs-asynchronous question rather than answering
it, and it is a real design pressure point.** Entry 40 settled that a
single-candidate synchronous request is *safe* — there is no hard wall-clock
limit for HTTP-triggered Workers and network waiting does not count toward CPU
time — and **that reasoning is unchanged and still correct.** What has changed
is the margin around the one caveat it recorded: a runtime update gives in-flight
requests a **30-second grace period**, and entry 40 judged a 20-second request's
collision with it *"very unlikely"*. **A request that spends 88 seconds in model
calls is not in that category.** It is still rare and still non-deterministic,
and it would still present as a dropped connection rather than an error body —
but the exposure now scales with a number that has been observed at four times
its previously assumed size.

**Two consequences, both recorded rather than acted on here.** A two-call chain
at the high end approaches or exceeds a full minute for **one** candidate, so
21.3's watchlist of 5–10 plus a trending pull is firmly a queued or streamed
design and not a parameter on a synchronous endpoint. And the eventual real
Derive endpoint should be designed knowing its p100 may be ~50 s, not ~20 s.

### ⚠ A NEW, UNANTICIPATED PATTERN IN HOW THE MODEL USED THE CITATION MECHANISM

**This is a finding, not a bug, and it is deliberately left unresolved.**

Two things the live runs did that nobody designed for:

1. **The model cited Stage 2's own claims (`assessment.claim.N`) as
   justification for Stage 3 parameters.** Those ids exist because Stage 3's
   prompt carries Stage 2's reasoning as citable evidence, which was intentional
   — but the intent was that a parameter would rest on a *datum* and the
   assessment would be *context*. It instead used the assessment as a premise.
2. **The model cited `news.status` — an ABSENCE marker — as justification for
   setting `takeProfitAmount` to `null`.**

**Both are technically valid under the current rules**, and that is precisely
what makes them worth recording. `news.status` is a real id this run really
emitted, carrying a real rendered value, and the citation check is a
set-membership test over emitted ids — which is exactly the guarantee step 37
argued for and got. Nothing malfunctioned.

But **"the news slot reports that no news was collected" being offered as
evidence FOR a parameter choice is a different act from citing a price**, and the
prompt's rule 5 says an absent input *"is UNKNOWN … do not treat its absence as
good news, bad news, or a quiet market"*. Citing an absence marker as grounds for
a null value sits in an unexamined gap between "the model correctly reported a
limitation" (which rule 5 requires, and which every prior probe did) and "the
model reasoned from a non-observation".

**OPEN QUESTION, for a later step and not settled here: should this be
tightened?** Options visible today, none evaluated: mark absence-reporting
evidence with a distinct id namespace or flag so citing it can be treated
differently; forbid absence markers as the *sole* citation for a value while
still allowing them alongside a real datum; or conclude it is working as intended
and that "I set no take-profit target because I have no news signal" is honest
reasoning a human should see. **It is flagged for review rather than resolved,
because changing the citation rules is a change to the safety mechanism itself
and deserves its own decision rather than a same-session judgement call.**

The first pattern deserves the same treatment: whether a parameter grounded in
another model's claim rather than in fetched data satisfies 21.5 requirement 1
is a real question, and it is **not** answered by "the id was real".

### ⚠ DERIVE CANNOT DETECT A BAD UPSTREAM JUDGEMENT

Carried forward from entry 40 deliberately, because Stage 3 is where it does the

Entry 40 found that thin or frozen sandbox data produces a confident-**looking**
Assess judgement that is financially meaningless: a series that sat at one price
for 83 minutes and then ~5 hours reads as a tight, stable range, which is exactly
the shape that makes "grid" look obviously right.

**Derive, handed such a judgement, faithfully turns it into concrete bounds, line
counts and order sizes that look equally plausible** — fully cited, passing every
decoder, every validator and every sanity bound, because it is doing its job
correctly on the inputs it was given. **Both live runs of this step are exactly
that**: mechanically correct, correctly grounded, and financially meaningless as
market advice.

**Nothing in Stage 3 can notice.** Every check answers *"is this parameter set
internally consistent, grounded in the fetched data, and acceptable to the real
create-bot validators?"* and **not one answers *"was the fetched data worth
reasoning about?"***. A well-grounded proposal derived from a meaningless
assessment is indistinguishable, by every mechanism this stage has, from one
derived from a good assessment.

**Derive can only be well grounded in whatever judgement it is given.** The human
in 21.1 is the only part of this pipeline that can tell the two apart. This is
stated in the folder README's banner, in three module headers and in
`index.ts`, and it is **tracked, not solved.**
