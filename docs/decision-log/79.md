# 79 — The grid ladder's `placed` latch: a halt/resume lifecycle that silently disabled any grid bot it touched

## PART 1 — ROOT CAUSE, AND WHY IT WAS NEVER NARROW

`GridLadder.placed` was a **one-way latch**. It is written in exactly two places
in the whole non-test tree: `false` by `emptyLadder`, whose only caller is
`createGrid`; and `true` by `#placeInitialLadder`, once, the first time a
placement pass completes with nothing throttled. **There was no third write.**

`decide`'s **first** gate was `if (!ladder.placed)`, and it was the **only**
condition in the system that could produce a `place_initial_ladder` action.

Every path that empties a ladder nulls the slots and leaves the latch alone:

| Path | Clears | Reset `placed`? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `#gridExit` (stop-loss, breakout, take-profit) | all slots | **No** |
| `liquidatePosition` | all slots | **No** |
| `#cancelOpenOrders`, reached from **every** `#halt` | every resolved rung | **No** |

`resume()` does not touch the ladder at all. `start()` requires `created` and is
unreachable for a halted bot.

**So the outcome was: empty slots, `placed: true`, `decide` returns `hold` on
every tick, forever.** Replace-on-fill cannot rescue it — that is driven by
fills, and there are no orders left to fill. The bot is genuinely `running`,
genuinely subscribed to its price feed, genuinely evaluating the market, and
genuinely incapable of placing a single order for the rest of its life.

### This was the normal lifecycle, not an edge case

`#halt` is reached from a **manual operator halt**, from **reconciliation's
`haltBot`**, from the **global kill switch**, from the **account circuit
breaker**, from **`order_rejected`**, and from **`unhandled_error`**. Since
`c180f08` gave `#cancelOpenOrders` its rung clear, every one of those paths ends
in a fully emptied ladder.

> **Any grid bot halted for any reason and later resumed was permanently inert.**
> Halting and resuming is the ordinary thing an operator does to a bot. The
> system's own recovery procedure was what disabled it.

Before `c180f08` the outcome was identical and merely better disguised: rungs
were left standing for orders already cancelled, so the ladder *displayed* as
populated while `#placeGridOrder`'s pre-send check refused every occupied level.
**`c180f08` converted a lying display into an honest one; it did not cause the
inertness.** The empty ladders the operator saw were the bug becoming visible,
not the bug arriving.

### The two real bots

- **`bot-qgo39d`** — liquidated earlier that night after a prior stuck-order
  incident, then resumed. `liquidatePosition`'s own clear emptied the ladder;
  `#applyGridExitFill` took `heldQuantity` to zero and left `placed` untouched.
- **`bot-gvtr1a`** — found split-brained by entry 77's new detector (D1 `halted`,
  object `running`), correctly halted to converge both stores, then resumed by
  the operator. **Its ladder was already empty before the detector ever ran** —
  entry 77's own PART 0 records "all eight slots null". The detector inherited an
  inert bot; it did not create one. Both it and the operator's resume behaved
  correctly.

### THE LESSON: a true finding, written down and left unconnected

This defect was described accurately, in plain English, **four separate times**
before anyone recognised it as a defect. Two of those predate tonight entirely:

| Where | Written | What it said |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ApplyMissedFillsAction.tsx` | **`7d7ad72`, 2026-07-31** | *"the rung comes back EMPTY, and nothing places it later either, because `#placeInitialLadder` only ever runs once (`placed: true`)"* |
| `reconciliation/findings.ts` | **`89044b8`, 2026-08-05** | *"It cannot self-heal, because `decide` only places a ladder when `placed` is false, so this state persists until a human acts."* |
| `liquidate-position.test.ts` | `09407ff`, tonight (entry 74) | *"`decide` rebuilds only while `placed` is false, so a resumed grid does not re-place its rungs."* |
| `docs/decision-log/77.md` PART 0 | tonight | *"It was inert only because its ladder was never rebuilt (all eight slots null)."* |

The **first two were written three and four weeks earlier, in unrelated
sessions**, by someone who had the whole mechanism in hand and stated it
correctly — as settled background for a different point. The entry-74 comment
went further and used it as a **premise** to set up an unrelated assertion. Entry
77 observed the consequence on a live bot and framed it as an incidental
explanation of why that bot was quiet.

**Four correct descriptions. None asked the next question: *then what places the
ladder after a wholesale clear?*** The finding was never missing. It was sitting
in the repository, in prose, unconnected, for four weeks.

## PART 2 — THE DESIGN DECISION: DERIVE THE CONDITION, AND WHY THE OBVIOUS FIX WAS REJECTED

### What was built

**The latch is kept, for its original and much narrower job.**
`#placeInitialLadder` leaves `placed` **false** whenever any order was throttled
or left unresolved, so the next tick re-enters and completes the levels that were
refused budget. That is a real contract, stated in that method's own header, and
it still works byte-for-byte.

**A second, independent condition is added beside it.** `decide`'s gate is now
`!ladder.placed || vacantLadder(ladder, outstanding)`. `vacantLadder` is a
genuine **five-part** state check, and every part is load-bearing:

| # | Conjunct | Why it is there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **every slot empty** | A partly populated ladder is a **working** ladder. Asking "is any level below spot empty" instead would fire on a healthy grid — a filled buy legitimately leaves its level empty while its replacement sell rests one level up — and re-placing there would put a second order against base already bought and already covered. |
| 2 | **no position held** (`heldQuantity == ZERO`) | Vacant **and holding** is `uncovered_held_inventory`'s condition. An initial ladder is **buys only**, so rebuilding there would resume trading around orphaned inventory and paper over the one detector written to find it. That is a human's decision. |
| 3 | **no order resting** (`openOrderIds` empty) | See below — this one has its own paragraph. |
| 4 | **no exit order live** (`exitOrderId === null`) | A resting liquidation or take-profit sell is disposing of a position; rebuilding beneath it would buy back what is being sold. |
| 5 | **no replacement queued** (`pendingReplacements` empty) | A queued replacement means the ladder is **mid-repair**, not dead. |

**A deviation, recorded because it is material.** The build brief summarised the
condition as three parts (slots, held quantity, queued replacements). The design
document's PART 3.2 specifies **five**, justifying each in its own table. The
build followed the **design document**, which the brief itself names as the
authority.

### Why `openOrderIds` is one of the five

`#cancelOpenOrders` deliberately **retains** the id of any cancellation the
exchange never confirmed, and of any record the fill-count gate refused to close.
`#gridExit`, separately, clears **every** rung wholesale. Those two combine into a
real, reachable state: **a flat bot with no rungs and an order that may still be
genuinely live on the exchange.**

Without conjunct 3, such a bot would place a **brand-new full ladder on top of an
order it cannot account for** — the exact class of hazard **entry 74** was built
to prevent, arrived at from the opposite direction. It has its own end-to-end
test (PART 7).

### REJECTED — reset the flag at each clear site

The obvious fix: add `placed: false` at the three sites that empty a ladder. It
works for bots halted *after* the fix, and it was rejected on four grounds, the
last decisive:

1. `#cancelOpenOrders` is not a wholesale clear in general — it retains rungs for
   unresolved orders, so resetting there is only safe by accident.
2. It leaves the latch a latch. A **fourth** clear site added later reintroduces
   the bug in full, silently.
3. It cannot reach the second exposure (PART 4), where no clear ever happens.
4. **It is forward-only.** `bot-qgo39d` and `bot-gvtr1a` **already carry
   `placed: true` in their permanent Durable Object storage**, written before any
   fix existed. Their clears are in the past. A fix that runs only *at clear time*
   would never run on either bot again — both would stay inert after deployment
   and need a manual clone-and-replace regardless.

The derived condition is evaluated **against live state on every tick**, which is
precisely why it reaches bots whose damage is already done.

### REJECTED — rebuild inside `resume()`, and the hazard tonight's own earlier fix exposed

Rebuilding the ladder directly in `#resumePass` is superficially the most direct
fix. It was rejected, and the reason is not style.

**Entry 77's part 3a — built earlier tonight — deliberately reordered `resume()`
so D1 is written FIRST and the object SECOND.** There is no transaction spanning
the two stores; an interruption is permanent by construction. What 3a chose was
which way the wreckage points: D1 `running` / object `halted`, a state where the
emergency sweeps still see the bot and — the load-bearing half — **nothing can
place an order**, because every order-placing site reads the object's own status.

**Placing a ladder inside `#resumePass` puts live exchange orders inside that
exact window.** An interruption after placement but before the object's status
write would leave N real resting orders belonging to a bot whose own state says
`halted` — a bot that will not poll them, will not fold their fills, and will not
cancel them. That is **strictly worse than the wreckage 3a was built to produce,
and it would be manufactured by the fix.**

> This is worth recording as a general finding, not a footnote: **one fix's design
> surfaced a real, concrete hazard in a second, independently reasonable-sounding
> fix.** Option (c) is what a competent engineer proposes first. It was
> disqualified only because 3a had been built hours earlier and its reasoning was
> written down where the next session could read it. Without entry 77, this fix
> would plausibly have gone in as (c) and partially undone it.

## PART 3 — THE GATE REORDER: LOAD-BEARING, NOT COSMETIC

**The rebuild check now runs AFTER the three risk exits, not before:**

| Before | After |
| --- | --- |
| 1. `!placed` → place | 1. stop-loss |
| 2. stop-loss | 2. breakout |
| 3. breakout | 3. take-profit |
| 4. take-profit | 4. `!placed \|\| vacant` → place |
| 5. hold | 5. hold |

**The concrete consequence of leaving it first.** `#gridExit` clears the *slots*.
It does **not** clear `realizedGross`. So a bot halted on take-profit is, the
moment it is resumed, **simultaneously vacant and still over its target**. With
the rebuild gate first, it would rebuild its entire ladder and re-trigger the
identical exit on the very next tick — **real orders placed and cancelled, every
cycle, forever, paying a spread each time round.** A bot halted on stop-loss and
resumed while price is still below the stop does exactly the same.

**Why the original ordering was defensible and stopped being so.** The comment
justifying placement-first argued: *"There is no risk exit to lose a race to
before any order exists."* That is an argument that placing first was
**harmless** — never that it was **necessary**. It was true only while the gate
could fire on a **brand-new bot and nothing else**. `vacantLadder` widens the gate
to a bot with **real history**: a position it has traded, profit it has realised,
an exit it has already taken. At that point "harmless" is simply false.

The move is also free, and provably so: all three exits read `ladder.levels` and
`ladder.realizedGross`, both built at creation and never a function of what is
resting. And it **improves** the fresh-bot case it was originally written for — a
bot created with spot already past its breakout now exits without first placing
and cancelling a full ladder.

## PART 4 — THE SECOND EXPOSURE, AND AN HONESTLY RESPECIFIED DETECTOR

### The second exposure, closed

`initialLadderOrders` breaks at the first level priced at or above spot. A grid
**started** while spot sits below its lowest line — but above its stop-loss, which
would otherwise have exited it — is handed an **empty list**. The old code ran the
loop zero times, found `throttled` still false, and **latched `placed: true`
having placed nothing.**

That is a bot advertising a ladder it never built, in `state.ladder.placed` — the
one field, mirrored to the API, that an operator reads to answer exactly that
question. It is now guarded: `if (!throttled && orders.length > 0)`.

**Found from source, never confirmed live, and this entry does not claim
otherwise.** The trading consequence is separately covered by `vacantLadder`
anyway; this guard is about the flag telling the truth, and about the exposure
staying closed if that condition is ever narrowed.

**It could not ship alone.** With placement still the *first* gate, refusing to
latch meant a fresh bot below its stop-loss would re-enter that gate every tick
with nothing to place and **never reach the stop-loss check at all**. The guard
and the reorder are one change.

### The detector, rejected in its obvious form

A reconciliation finding for "running, `placed`, zero slots" was considered and
**rejected**, because this project's own alert tiers cannot express it:

- **`meaningful`** alerts **and calls `haltBot`** — it would **immediately re-halt
  the very bot the fix exists to revive**, and require another operator resume.
- **`minor`** auto-corrects and logs and **never alerts** — silent by
  construction, which defeats the purpose.
- The one escape (`scope !== "bot"` alerts without halting) would require lying
  about the scope of a plainly bot-scoped finding.

### What was built instead

A **standing, self-resolving alert** — `grid_ladder_vacant`, `warning`, raised
through `#raiseStanding` in the same shape as `grid_replacement_queued` — on the
bot's **own price-processing path**, at the single site that both knows a rebuild
was attempted and knows whether it actually placed anything.

Two properties that site buys, neither incidental:

- **No false positive on a healthy resume.** A legitimately resumed bot is
  `running` with a vacant ladder until its first price tick lands. A
  snapshot-reading observer would alert on **every** healthy resume. Reached from
  the price path, the condition is only ever evaluated *after* a real price
  evaluation declined to place.
- **Both halves in one owner.** `alerts/standing.ts` is explicit that raising
  without resolving is **worse than an unconditional insert** — one row that never
  clears suppresses every future alert of that type for that bot, forever. The
  resolve half normally lives in the poll; putting the raise on the price path and
  the resolve in the poll would have **split ownership** of one incident between
  two writers that see different things. Both halves run at the one site instead,
  and `grid_ladder_vacant` is deliberately **not** added to
  `POLL_STANDING_ALERT_TYPES` — the poll never evaluates it and must not close it.

## PART 5 — MUTATION TESTING FOUND THREE GENUINE DEFENCE-IN-DEPTH CONDITIONS

**Three of eleven mutants survived their first run: M9, M10 and M11, each
deleting one term of `gridOutstanding` (conjuncts 3, 4 and 5).**

They were **not** accepted as coverage gaps, and they were **not** papered over
with a contrived test. The session investigated what each term actually protects
and found that **two of the three conditions cannot occur independently on a real
bot**:

- **`exitOrderId`** is only ever set for a quantity that was *held* — `#gridExit`
  places a liquidation sell **only** when `heldQuantity > ZERO` — and is cleared
  by the same fill that takes the position flat.
- **`pendingReplacements`** is only ever populated because a buy filled and its
  paired sell could not be placed, which means base **is** held.

In both cases **conjunct 2 already refuses the rebuild on its own.** These are
genuine **defence in depth**: correct, deliberate, and structurally redundant in
every state this system can actually produce. Testing them through a bot would
have meant contriving state the system cannot reach.

**This was proven, not assumed.** `gridOutstanding` was **exported** and all three
terms tested **directly against the contract the function states**, rather than
against a fixture that cannot express them. The reasoning is written into the
function's own header so the next reader does not have to re-derive it.

**The third, `openOrderIds`, IS independently reachable** — the `#gridExit`
+ unconfirmed-cancellation combination described in PART 2 — and it got a real
**end-to-end** test, not a contract test.

All three now die. The distinction is the point: two are redundancy that is
correct to keep and honest to label; one was a real gap and was closed as one.

## PART 6 — A SELF-CAUGHT DEFECT IN TWO PRE-EXISTING TESTS

While building this fix, two **existing** tests in `grid.test.ts` were found to
have been testing the wrong thing all along.

Their shared helper, `placedLadder()`, is `{ ...emptyLadder(params), placed: true }`
— **`placed: true` with every slot null**, which is **precisely the dead-ladder
shape this entire entry is about.** The two tests using it read:

- *"holds a placed ladder that is inside its bounds"*
- *"leaves the bot idle above the ladder when breakoutTakeProfit is off"*

Both **claimed** to assert the behaviour of an ordinary working grid. Both were in
fact handing `decide` a ladder that no longer had a single order on it. Under the
new gate they stopped asserting `hold` and started exercising the **rebuild
path** — which is how they were found.

Fixed with a new `workingLadder()` helper carrying a genuinely live rung, so the
tests now assert what their names always said. **Found and corrected by this
session's own diligence; nothing else flagged it**, and the design document had
anticipated the helper would need extending (its PART 8) without knowing two
existing assertions were already wrong.

## PART 8 — THE OPERATOR'S LIVE CONFIRMATION OF `bot-gvtr1a`

**This session made no live calls of any kind.** Everything below was read by the
**operator**, independently, against real D1 and the real API, and is recorded
here because the design explicitly flagged it as the one thing source could not
settle.

**[OPERATOR]** Queried `bot-gvtr1a` directly and confirmed **both** diagnostic
conditions the design named as necessary to check:

- **`state.ladder.heldQuantity` reads exactly zero.**
- **`state.ladder.placed` reads `true`.**

**Why both mattered.** The design's PART 7 stated plainly that self-correction is
**conditional on the bot being flat** — `vacantLadder` refuses to rebuild while
base is held, deliberately, because an initial ladder is buys-only and would trade
around uncovered inventory. Whether `bot-gvtr1a` held base was **not knowable from
source**, and entry 77 records that the detector's halt preserves a position for a
human. It does not hold one. **It is the straightforward case.**

And `placed: true` is the **confirmation of the diagnosis itself**. The design
said: *if this reads `false` on either bot, the diagnosis is wrong and the build
session should stop and re-investigate* — because a `false` flag would have
re-placed on its own. It reads `true`. **This is the latch, on a real bot, and not
a different problem wearing the same symptoms.**

**[OPERATOR] A pleasant secondary confirmation, independent of this entry's fix.**
`bot-gvtr1a`'s own alert history shows **the exact sequence entry 77's 3a/3b/3c
was built to produce** — a drift detection, followed by a converging halt, **both
now resolved**. That is tonight's split-brain work observed working live, for the
**second** time tonight, on a bot it was designed from. It says nothing about this
entry's fix and is not offered as evidence for it.

## PART 9 — NOT DONE / NOT VERIFIED

- **NOTHING IN THIS FIX HAS BEEN DEPLOYED. NOTHING HAS BEEN EXERCISED LIVE.**
  Every claim about behaviour rests on the local suite and on reading source.
- **`bot-qgo39d` and `bot-gvtr1a` are EXPECTED, not OBSERVED, to self-correct.**
  The prediction is that on the first price tick after deployment, no risk exit
  applying, `vacantLadder` is true and the ladder is placed — with no resume, no
  button and no clone. That expectation is now backed by a **live-confirmed
  starting state for `bot-gvtr1a`** (PART 8), which removes the one conditional
  the design could not close. **It remains a prediction until checked
  post-deploy.** `bot-qgo39d`'s flatness is expected from source
  (`#applyGridExitFill` zeroes `heldQuantity` on the exit's full fill) but has
  **not** been confirmed by an operator read.
- **A separate, explicit, human-triggered "force a ladder rebuild" action was
  designed and deliberately NOT built.** It is option (d) in the design document,
  and it is the answer for the one case automatic rebuild refuses:
  **vacant-and-holding**. Correctly not needed here — `bot-gvtr1a` is confirmed
  flat and `bot-qgo39d` is expected to be — but it is a real, specified, available
  option the moment a bot is found holding a position in this state. Recorded as
  an open item, not as a gap.
- **The second exposure (PART 4) has never been confirmed live**, only from
  source. This entry does not propose reproducing it live.
- **The source-scan tripwire proves only that someone was made to look**, not that
  a new clear site is handled. Its own header says so.
- **The three defence-in-depth terms (PART 5) are redundant in every state this
  system can currently reach.** If a future change makes one of them
  independently reachable, its contract test will still hold but the end-to-end
  coverage will not exist.
- **NOTHING IN THIS ENTRY IS COMMITTED AS OF WRITING.** `HEAD` is `def1d68`. Eight
  files sit modified or new on disk, plus the design document, plus this entry and
  its README row.
