## Step 20: Arming the poll — the shared standing-alert mechanism, and the alarm
Date: 2026-08-03

Step 19 built `#pollOpenOrders` and deliberately did not schedule it. This step
schedules it, at 30 seconds, on a `BotInstance` alarm — and does the refactor
step 19 recorded as an explicit **precondition** first, because arming the timer
without it would have recreated step 18's duplicate-alert problem at roughly 60x
the rate the 5-minute cron produced it at.

### The precondition, done first

`raiseStandingAlert` was module-private to `reconcile.ts`. Reusing it from the
Durable Object had exactly two options, and only one of them is a refactor: move
the mechanism, or write a second one. A second one would have passed its own
tests — that is the whole difficulty — right up until the two lifecycles
disagreed about what "the same alert" is. That is the failure mode
`shared/alert-types.ts` already exists to prevent for the alert-type *format*
(18.1's addendum), applied here to the alert *lifecycle*.

**`/src/alerts/standing.ts` is the one implementation**, and both halves moved,
not just the raise. Step 18 recorded why they cannot be separated: dedup without
resolution is *strictly worse* than an unconditional insert — one row that never
clears, suppressing every future alert of that kind for that bot, forever.
Handing the new caller only the half it needed first would have handed it that
bug.

`reconcile.ts` keeps two thin adapters (`raiseFindingAlert`,
`resolveClearedAlerts`) supplying what is genuinely reconciliation's: every
finding it raises is `critical`/`trading`, it owns
`reconciliation_{tier}_{kind}` rows, and its scope is the account's bots. Its
behaviour is byte-for-byte unchanged, which the existing step-18 tests pin.

**Not `/src/shared`**, and the reason is that folder's own contract rather than
taste: nothing in it performs I/O, reads a clock, or touches storage, and
`alert-types.ts` earns its place there by being dependency-free so the
dashboard's separate toolchain (the deliberate TS 5.7/7 split) compiles it
unchanged. This module writes D1 rows and takes a `Database`. The boundary it
crosses — a cron Worker and a Durable Object — is inside one bundle and one
toolchain, so that import costs nothing and the dashboard never sees the file.
`/src/alerts` is the recording half of section 10, the counterpart to
`/src/notifications`, whose README has always said it writes no alert row.

### The one real judgement call: `source` scopes both halves

Step 18 states the incident key as `(alert_type, bot_instance_id)` — the
cooldown's own key, so the recording and notifying layers agree. The
implementation also filtered on `source`, and the question this step had to
settle is whether that stays now that there are two writers.

It stays, and it is **ownership rather than a second key**. A writer may only
close an incident it was in a position to observe; a writer that cannot resolve
a row must therefore not deduplicate against it either, or it would suppress its
own alert behind a row it can never clear. Both halves scope identically, which
is the actual invariant. The visible consequence is that reconciliation and the
poll would keep separate rows for a shared `alert_type` — a case that does not
exist today, since no type is written by both.

The alternative (dedup globally on `(alert_type, bot_instance_id)`) is a
one-line change if that is ever wanted, but it needs the resolution question
answered first, and "whoever notices first closes it" is not an answer for a
condition only one of them can see.

### What the poll owns, and what it deliberately does not

Three types: `unattributable_fill` (step 19's, now on the standing path),
`poll_blind` and `poll_blind_escalated` (new). Everything else this object
writes stays an unconditional insert, and that is right for them:
`unknown_order_fill`, `order_state_drift`, `cancel_failed`,
`cancel_fill_discrepancy` and the rest mark **discrete events** at the moment
they happen. Nothing re-derives them on a schedule, so they cannot accumulate.

Three of them are also reconciliation's to ingest and close
(`INGESTED_ALERT_TYPES`), and a poll that claimed them would close incidents it
never observed. Worse, it would do so invisibly: 18.1 gates the "Apply missed
fills" control on an **unresolved** drift row, so a poll resolving
`order_state_drift` would make the repair control vanish from a bot that still
needs it — the same silent-gap-on-a-correction-surface failure 18.1's addendum
refused to leave in a backlog. `POLL_STANDING_ALERT_TYPES` is a closed set for
that reason, and a test pins it.

### The alarm: one, multiplexed, from day one

`PriceFeed`'s own lesson, not a general principle borrowed from elsewhere: a
Durable Object has exactly one alarm, so two concerns each calling `setAlarm`
means the second silently cancels the first. There is one scheduled concern
today, and it is written as if there were several — `#nextAlarmAt` folds every
concern into the single instant, `alarm()` checks whether the poll is actually
due rather than assuming it is why the object woke, and `#armAlarm` reconciles
against `getAlarm()` instead of blindly setting.

**Arming hangs off `#putState`.** That is the single choke point every
`openOrderIds` mutation already passes through — placement, fills, cancellation,
the ladder, halt, close. Arming at each of those instead works exactly until
someone adds a seventh and forgets, and the failure is silent: a bot with a
resting order and no timer, which is precisely the condition step 19 exists to
end.

**The firing is consumed before the pass runs.** `nextPollAt` is cleared first,
because everything the pass writes goes through `#putState`, which re-arms — and
re-arming from the instant that has just fired sets an alarm in the past, which
fires immediately, forever. A mutant that skips the clearing fails four tests.

Status handling, per step 19's finding: `halted` is **still polled**, because
observing costs nothing and a halt whose cancellation failed leaves live orders
on the exchange while a human is deciding about exactly those books. What it
must never do is place a replacement, which is not re-defended here — step 19
derives `placeReplacement` from `status === "running"` inside the poll itself.
`created` has no open orders and so arms nothing. `stopped` is excluded
explicitly even though `close()` empties `openOrderIds`, because
`checkOpenOrders` refuses a stopped bot outright and a poll would be work whose
result nothing may use.

**`alarm()` never throws.** An alarm has no caller to report to, and a throwing
handler is retried by the runtime on its own schedule — which would double up on
a poll that is already failing. An object holding no config or unreadable state
(an alarm can outlive a bot that was never finished; step 6, open question 6)
disarms and returns rather than escalating.

### Backoff and blindness, mirroring the feed

30s healthy; on an unreadable pass the delay doubles to a 5-minute floor
(60/120/240/300). After five consecutive unreadable passes the poll is blind:
one `warning` `poll_blind`, then one `critical` `poll_blind_escalated` if it
stays blind past 30 minutes, then a slow retry that keeps going so a long outage
self-heals rather than dying silently. That is `PriceFeed`'s policy, chosen
because it is the closest precedent in this system for "a thing that should be
watching has stopped being able to".

Both go through the **standing** path, which the feed's do not. Belt and braces
on purpose: the `blindSince`/`escalated` flags already prevent a re-raise, but
they are this object's own bookkeeping, and if they were ever reset while the
condition persisted the alert would begin repeating at the poll's cadence. The
shared dedup makes that impossible independently of them — and, more usefully,
gives both alerts a real **resolution** the moment a pass reads cleanly.

Two smaller decisions inside that:

- **A successful pass clears the blind state in `#observeOpenOrders`, not in the
  alarm**, so a human's `checkOpenOrders` also ends the episode. Keeping the
  flags and the rows in lockstep matters: a resolution that left the flags set
  would mean `poll_blind` could never be raised again.
- **Disarming does not resolve `poll_blind`.** When `openOrderIds` empties the
  failure count resets — a bot with nothing to poll is not failing to poll — but
  only a pass that actually read cleanly may close the row. "There is nothing to
  read any more" is not evidence that reading works, and the orders may have
  left `openOrderIds` precisely because this object's view of them is wrong.
- **A failed pass clears `nextPollAt` as well as incrementing the count**, found
  while re-reading the interaction rather than from a failing test. A pass that
  reads some orders and fails on others *writes state*, and that write re-arms
  through `#putState` at the healthy 30 seconds — so without clearing, the
  backoff would silently not apply to exactly the mixed case, and a bot able to
  read one order but not another would poll the unreadable one every 30 seconds
  forever. Now covered by a test and a mutant.

`skipped` is deliberately **not** what the lifecycle is gated on. It also holds
fills that were read perfectly well and then refused (an unattributable
quantity, a fill the state machine would not accept), so gating on it would keep
`poll_blind` open for as long as an `unattributable_fill` stood — conflating
"we cannot see" with "we can see something wrong". The pass counts reads and
failed reads separately for that reason.

### A test-infrastructure fault this step exposed

Arming a real alarm surfaced something that had been latent: **the pool does
fire Durable Object alarms**, and every fake clock in the suite was set in the
past (`1_760_000_000_000` is October 2025). So every armed alarm was already
overdue the instant it was written, and the runtime raced each test with a
spurious poll — sometimes against the test's injected clock and exchange,
sometimes against a re-created instance with neither. It first appeared as two
contradictory failures in the same run: an alarm that read `null` and an alarm
holding a real-world 2026 timestamp.

Fixed by moving the clock origin forward in the eight test files that drive a
real `BotInstance` (`FakeExchange.now` moved with it, since the two were equal
by construction and the trade-timestamp assertions rely on it). Every assertion
in those files is relative to `T0`, so this cost nothing. Recorded because it is
not obvious, and because the suite passing before this step was luck rather than
isolation.

### Tests

**41 new (1453 → 1494)**: 19 in the new `src/alerts/standing.test.ts`, 22 in
`bot-instance.test.ts`.

The mechanism tests are the ordinary half. The half worth recording is the
cross-caller one, and it took two attempts:

**The first version proved nothing.** It gave each writer its own bot, and every
cross-writer assertion in it passed *with the ownership checks deleted* — the
bot-scoping alone kept them apart. Rewritten so both writers hold open incidents
against the **same** `bot_instance_id`, which is also what production looks
like: reconciliation reconciles the very account its bots are polling. One
resting order, reported fully filled with no per-fill detail, read by the real
poll as an unattributable fill and by the real cron as meaningful order-state
drift. Five cron passes and ten poll passes produce one row each; interleaving
them changes nothing.

The resolution half is asserted the same way: a *stale* reconciliation-owned
incident is planted on the same bot, so one pass closes one row and leaves the
other — "run a pass and check nothing happened" would have passed just as well
if the resolve half had never run at all.

Nine mutants, all caught:

| mutant | result |
| --- | --- |
| `raiseStandingAlert` inserts unconditionally | 8 failures |
| the shared `observed` gate removed | 2 |
| the poll's own `observedEverything` gate removed | 5 |
| resolve ignores `source` | 1 |
| the poll claims ownership of every alert type | 1 |
| the poll runs on a `stopped` bot | 1 |
| the alarm never disarms | 2 |
| `unattributable_fill` raised through `#alert` again | 7 |
| a failed pass leaves `nextPollAt` set (no backoff on a mixed pass) | 1 |

The fifth is the one that **survived the first time it was tried**, on the
first-version cross-caller test above, and is the reason that test was rewritten
and a further one added: a real `cancel_failed` on the polled bot must survive
any number of clean passes. Without it, `POLL_STANDING_ALERT_TYPES` could have
been widened to everything and nothing would have failed.

A source-level guard backs the behavioural ones, in the shape
`no-raw-d1.test.ts` established: the raise/resolve pair is declared in exactly
one file, and every one of the poll's re-detected conditions is raised through
`#raiseStanding` rather than `#alert`.

Backend `tsc --noEmit` clean; dashboard `tsc -b && vite build` clean (unchanged
— nothing the dashboard imports moved).

### Deliberately not done

- **No pipeline integration.** Poll results are not wired into `onPriceUpdate`
  or the strategy decision path, and making that interleaving safe is step 21's
  entire subject. The alarm and the price feed can now both reach this object
  concurrently, which is exactly why that question gets its own step rather than
  riding along here.
- **No dashboard surface** for the poll, its schedule, or the two new alert
  types. They appear in the existing alert feed like any other row.
- **Not deployed, and not run against the live bots.** The suite drives it; no
  testnet object has been armed.
- **`applyMissedFills` and the halt-sweep interaction still untouched**, at the
  operator's standing direction (step 19).

### Open questions

1. **The 30-second interval and the 5-failure blind threshold are still
   decisions, not measurements** (step 19's open question 3, unchanged, now with
   a second number attached to it). Four bots make the rate-limit cost trivial:
   one `getOrderStatus` per open order at `routine` priority. A grid ladder with
   many rungs is the case that changes it — a full ladder polled every 30
   seconds is a materially different draw on the budget, and nothing measures
   that yet.
2. **`poll_blind` can outlive the condition it describes.** Disarming does not
   resolve it (see above), so a bot whose orders all closed while its reads were
   failing keeps an open warning until it places another order and polls
   cleanly. Deliberately the safe direction, but it means the row can be stale
   in a way `unattributable_fill`'s cannot.
3. **The two writers are kept apart by three independent checks** (`source`,
   `owns`, `inScope`) and only two of them have a mutant that isolates them
   today. That is defence in depth working as intended, but it does mean a
   single-layer regression is not always visible as a test failure.
4. Step 19's open question 1 (the 0.00031777 BTC with no in-system home) and
   18's open questions 2 and 3 all stand.
