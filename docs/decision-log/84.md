## PART 1 — WHAT TRIGGERED THIS, AND THE CORRECTION IT FORCES

A live diagnostic measured where Durable Object time actually goes:

| Source | Share of measured time |
| --- | --- |
| `BotInstance.alarm` | **52.5%** |
| `RateLimiter.acquire` | **36.6%** |
| `PriceFeed` | **under 1%** |

### The correction, stated explicitly so it is not lost

**Every billing investigation in this cycle up to now assumed the cost was
`PriceFeed`. That assumption was wrong, and this entry corrects it.**

[Entry 81](81.md) ("The PriceFeed compute-duration leak") found and fixed a real
bug — feeds that stayed awake with nothing listening — and **nothing in that
entry is retracted**. What is corrected is the **baseline assumption its framing
carried forward**: that `PriceFeed` was therefore *the* cost centre, and that
further cost work belonged there. It was not, and it does not. `PriceFeed` is now
**under 1%** of measured time. The remaining **89.1%** sits in the two objects
this entry addresses, and neither had been looked at.

The lesson is the ordinary one and worth writing down: **entry 81 found a leak by
reasoning about a mechanism, not by measuring the distribution.** A found bug is
not evidence that it was the dominant cost. One diagnostic pass over the whole
system corrected a month of misdirected attention.

## PART 2 — `bot-mp3v95`: HEALTHY, NOT A BUG

The diagnostic flagged one bot as an outlier: **780ms average `alarm()`, against
250–310ms across its 16 peers, max 2,186ms**, on what the report described as the
same config shape.

**The config shape was the same. The open-order count was not.**

| | `bot-mp3v95` | All 16 peers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Open orders | **4** | **1** each |

That is the entire finding. `#pollOpenOrders` looped over `openOrderIds`
sequentially, one `getOrderStatus` per order, each preceded by its own
`RateLimiter.acquire` RPC — **two network round trips per open order, in series**.
Cost was therefore linear in open-order count, and 4 orders against 1 is the
whole of the 780ms against 250–310ms. Fitting `F + N·C` to both ends gives
**C ≈ 175ms per order, F ≈ 80ms fixed**, consistent at both.

The **2,186ms max** is the rate limiter, not the venue: `#gate` sleeps and
re-presents its ticket when the account budget is exhausted
(`rate-limited.ts:308`). All 17 armed bots share one `gemini-main` budget, and
`bot-mp3v95` drew **4 tickets per pass** — the largest single consumer, with four
independent chances of being told to wait where a peer had one.

**Everything else about the bot was clean.** `audit_log` held exactly two rows for
its entire life (`capital.allocated`, `bot.started`, both 2026-08-05 06:49) — not
one `bot.open_orders_checked` in 26 days, meaning no pass ever applied a fill,
closed an order, or refused one. Its 22 alerts were all `resolved=1`. Its 13
`poll_blind` / 6 `poll_blind_escalated` — the fleet's highest — are a
**consequence of the same 4x**, not a separate fault: a pass fails if *any* read
fails, so four sequential reads carry roughly four times a peer's chance of a
failed pass at the same per-read failure rate. Every underlying cause was
infrastructure shared fleet-wide (`Durable Object reset because its code was
updated`, `Network connection lost`, two internal storage resets with Cloudflare
references).

Its four orders were the bottom four rungs of a DOGEUSD grid ladder, placed in one
batch and never touched: `created_at` and `updated_at` identical at 2026-08-05
06:50, `filled_quantity` 0, still `pending` 26 days later. **DOGEUSD does not fill
under sandbox conditions.** The operator has since halted and archived the bot;
that is unrelated to this code and to this change.

**One correction made during the investigation:** an early reading of the order
table appeared to show all four orders sharing one `exchange_order_id`, which
would have been a placement bug. It was a display artifact of truncating the ids
to a shared 14-character prefix. The ids are distinct
(`73798699084641051`–`...054`). Recorded because the false reading was briefly
acted on.

## PART 3 — CHANGE 1: CONCURRENT ORDER READS

`#pollOpenOrders` split into a **concurrent read phase** and a **sequential apply
phase**.

```
before:  for (id of openOrderIds) { await getOrderStatus(id); ...apply... }
after:   await Promise.all(openOrderIds.map(getOrderStatus))
         ...classify...
         for ({id, remote} of readable) { ...apply... }
```

### Why only the reads

**The application must stay sequential and in order.**
`#applyGridFillToOrder` places the paired sell for a filled buy, mutating the
ladder and `openOrderIds`; the inner fill loop re-reads `#order()` and `#state()`
on every iteration *because the previous fill mutated them*. Fanning the writes
out would race the ladder against itself. Only the reads are independent, so only
the reads are parallel.

### The RateLimiter was traced, not assumed

The brief required checking rather than wrapping and hoping. **`acquire()` is safe
under concurrency by construction.** Its whole decision path — `ceilingFor` →
`usedWeight` → `#claimedAhead` → `WeightBudget.consume` — is **one synchronous
run**, and `consume` (`shared/rate-limiter.ts:215`) contains no `await`. The only
`await` in `acquire` is `#persist()`, which happens **after** the weight is
already spent in memory. A Durable Object yields only at an await, so two
concurrent acquires cannot pass the same check.

This is stated as an invariant by the method itself at
**`rate-limiter.ts:283`** — *"If it were the other way round — check, await the
write, then record — two `acquire` calls could both pass the check while the first
was suspended on its storage write, and both be granted the same weight"* — and
`rate-limiter.test.ts:399` **forces that interleaving deterministically** rather
than reasoning about it. Each caller queues its own ticket via
`#issue`/`#resolveTicket`, so N concurrent readers are indistinguishable from N
separate bots on the account, a case the queue already handles. `bot-instance.ts:1455`
records that step 21's probe had **already observed two `getOrderStatus` calls
outstanding at once** here from concurrent re-entry. **Nothing in `RateLimiter`
was changed.**

`Promise.all` also preserves throw semantics exactly: it attaches handlers to
every promise immediately, so a sibling rejection cannot surface as an unhandled
rejection, and the first rejection propagates to `#runScheduledPoll`'s catch just
as a mid-loop throw did.

### A green suite could not prove this change did anything

The **results** are identical either way; only the timing differs. So the suite
passing at 3336 → 3337 proved nothing about the concurrency. A timing-
discriminating test was added, and **verified against the old code**:

| | max concurrent reads |
| --- | --- |
| Original sequential loop | **1** — fails |
| After this change | **2** — passes |

It also asserts the fan-out preserves `openOrderIds` order. It lives in
`grid-bot-instance.test.ts` because the ladder is the only strategy that produces
two simultaneous open orders — the same reason the existing mixed-pass test is
there.

### The one behavioral side effect

**Unreadable-order classification now runs *before* the deferral check.**

`#runScheduledPoll`'s own comment documents the mixed case — *"a pass that failed
to read order A, then deferred on order B"* — as one whose failure **must
survive**, because it is real evidence the venue is unreachable and dropping it
suppresses the backoff and `poll_blind` for exactly the bot that needs them. The
sequential loop only ever knew about unreadable orders **it happened to reach
before deferring**. The new shape classifies all of them first.

**This is strictly more correct against that existing comment, not a regression.**
A secondary, cosmetic consequence: entries within `skipped` now group
unreadable-first rather than interleaving by position. Contents and counts are
unchanged, nothing reads it positionally, and the existing mixed-pass test passes
untouched.

## PART 4 — CHANGE 2: CONDITIONAL POLL INTERVAL

The flat 30-second alarm replaced by a tier derived from state:

| Tier | Condition | Interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **urgent** | `exitOrderId !== null`, **or** `pendingReplacements` non-empty | **30s** |
| **grid** | grid, running | **45s** |
| **routine** | dca / trailing_stop, no exit in flight | **60s** |
| **dormant** | halted | **120s** |

### Why 30s was oversampling

**The alarm never evaluates stop-loss or take-profit.** Those run in
`#onPriceUpdatePass` through `decide()` — a pure function of
`(config, position, price, hasOpenOrder)` — driven by price ticks from
`PriceFeed`. The alarm never calls `decide()`. Step 21 settled this deliberately:
a poll-observed fill does not act, because the poll has no price and every action
either strategy can take needs one.

So a slower poll does not slow any risk control directly. It slows **fill
detection**, which reaches a decision only through the next tick — and the feed's
measured cadence is **one closed candle every 35–70s, ~130s worst case** (the
figure that sized `PRICE_STALENESS_MS`). A 30-second poll was sampling roughly
**twice as fast as the signal that can act on what it finds**.

60s keeps the worst case fill-to-decision path at ~130s — **exactly the gap
between two closed candles this system already tolerates**. That parity is the
reason not to go further: past 60s the poll stops being the fast half and becomes
the binding constraint.

### THE CRITICAL FINDING — the first version was structurally unsafe

**Three safety tests were written and passing BEFORE the tiering logic existed.**
They assert that an exit in flight or a queued replacement holds the tightest
interval regardless of strategy or status. Against the flat 30s they passed
trivially. **That was the point: a ratchet in place before the change that could
break it.**

**One of them went genuinely red during implementation, and that is what caught
the gap.**

`#syncAlarm` recomputes `nextPollAt` **only when it is null** — i.e. after a
firing. So a bot armed on a loose tier **holds that instant even after becoming
urgent**. And the transitions that matter arrive on the **price-tick path**, not
the poll: a fill whose replacement could not be placed, or a stop-loss placing a
liquidation sell. **There is no firing in between to re-arm it.**

> **Up to a full loose interval — as much as 120s — could pass with an uncovered
> position, or an in-flight exit unmonitored.**

**Fix: a tighten-only ratchet on `#syncAlarm`.** It pulls the next poll in
immediately when conditions become urgent, and **never pushes it out** outside a
normal firing. A tier that relaxes just costs one extra poll — the safe direction
to be wrong in — and it cannot oscillate, because once pulled in, `now + delay`
grows past the instant it just set.

### The ordering, and proof the ratchet bites

The two urgent checks come **first, ahead of the status check**, and that order is
the whole safety of `pollTierFor`. A grid stop-loss cancels every rung, places a
liquidation sell, and **halts** — leaving a bot that is `halted` **and** mid-exit.
Testing status first puts exactly that bot on the dormant interval, slowing the
poll for the single most important resting order in the system.

**This was verified by deliberately breaking it.** Reordering `pollTierFor` to
check status before urgency — the realistic way this regresses — produced:

```
expected 120000 to be 30000
```

A bot mid-liquidation demoted to the dormant tier, **caught by the safety test
written in step 1**. The correct ordering was then restored.

### `ladder !== undefined` as the grid test

Sound because it is only consulted for an **armed** bot: `#pollArmed` requires a
resting order, a grid's resting orders are placed by its ladder, and no other
strategy writes the key. It is also **the one derivation here that can be wrong
without being dangerous** — misreading a strategy moves 45s to 60s and nothing
else. The two urgent conditions are read **directly from the fields that define
them**, with no derivation in between. Derive where being wrong is cheap; read
directly where it is not.

**120s for halted** is the midpoint of the proposed 120–300s range. 300s was
rejected as too stale for an operator actively reviewing books — which is the
entire reason halted bots are polled at all (step 19). 120s still removes three
quarters of the firings and leaves the backoff room to climb 120 → 240 → the cap.

Backoff now doubles **the tier's** base rather than a global 30s, so a bot always
backs off from wherever it actually sits.

## PART 5 — RESULTS

### Fleet-wide, 17 armed bots (12 DCA routine, 4 grid, 1 halted)

| | Before | After | Change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Alarm firings / day | 48,960 | **25,680** | **−48%** |
| Exchange reads / day | 57,600 | **31,440** | **−45%** |

The two changes are complementary and do not overlap: **change 1 cuts duration
per alarm; change 2 cuts the number of alarms.** On `bot-mp3v95` they compounded
— **780ms → ~250–310ms** per alarm, and **2,880 → 1,920** firings per day.

Tier assignment above is inferred from strategy/status in D1; `exitOrderId` and
`pendingReplacements` live in DO storage and are not visible from there, so a bot
currently in an urgent state would sit at 30s and this **slightly understates**.

### Tests

| | Files | Tests |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Session baseline | 123/124 | **3336** |
| Final | 123/124 | **3346 passed (3346)** |

**10 new:** 1 concurrency-timing, 3 safety-ratchet, 6 tier-matrix.

**8 pre-existing tests updated**, and the two categories are different:

- **7 DCA alarm tests hardcoded the old flat 30s.** Every expected value was
  exactly 2x under the routine tier — these encoded old behavior, not breakage.
  Rewritten against an imported `POLL_TIER_INTERVAL_MS.routine` **so a future
  tier change reads as an intentional tier change rather than a mystery number

`api/serialize.ts` and `research/derive-prompt.ts`), **none in any touched file**.
The single suite "error" is the pre-existing Miniflare worker-teardown artifact,
present on baseline.
